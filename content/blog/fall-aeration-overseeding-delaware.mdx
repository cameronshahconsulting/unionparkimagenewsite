**If a Delaware lawn gets exactly one treatment a year, it should be core aeration plus overseeding in September or early October.** No single service does more to thicken thin turf, and no timing works better — warm soil germinates seed fast, cool air stops it frying, and next spring's weeds aren't around yet to compete. Here's why this works so well here specifically, and what it costs.

## The Delaware lawn problem in one paragraph

Almost every lawn in New Castle County is tall fescue — a cool-season grass growing on compacted clay. Fescue loves our spring and fall and merely *survives* our July. Every summer thins it a little; clay compaction means water, air, and roots struggle a little more each year; and thin turf invites crabgrass to fill the gaps. Left alone, the lawn slowly loses the war. Aeration and overseeding is how you reverse the direction — you re-thicken the lawn faster than summer thins it.

## What actually happens

**Core aeration** pulls thousands of finger-sized soil plugs out of the lawn. That relieves compaction, opens channels for water and air, and — critically — exposes bare soil for seed contact.

**Overseeding** spreads quality tall fescue seed (blends matter; contractor-grade "shady mix" from a big box store is how you get a weedy, mismatched lawn) across the lawn immediately after, so seed falls into the aeration holes — a near-perfect seedbed.

Done together in fall, germination hits in 7–14 days, and the new grass gets all of fall plus all of the following spring to root before facing its first Delaware summer.

## Why spring seeding disappoints

Spring-seeded fescue faces crabgrass pre-emergent conflicts (the herbicide that stops crabgrass also stops your grass seed), rising heat 8–10 weeks after germination, and summer fungus pressure on baby grass. It can work; it usually underwhelms. September seed enters July as a nine-month-old plant with real roots. May seed enters July as a two-month-old seedling. One of them survives.

## Protecting the investment (the part people skip)

1. **Water lightly, daily, for 2–3 weeks.** New seed that dries out once is dead seed. This is the single point of failure.
2. **Mow normally once new grass hits 3.5–4 inches** — sharp blade, never below 3 inches.
3. **Skip fall pre-emergents** on seeded areas; they'll block your grass too.
4. **Leave the plugs.** The aeration cores dissolve back into the lawn in a couple of weeks and feed the soil.
5. **Feed in late fall.** A November fertilization is the best-timed feeding of the entire year for fescue roots.
