**The most reliable low-maintenance plants for Delaware yards are inkberry holly, oakleaf hydrangea, itea (Virginia sweetspire), catmint, coneflower, black-eyed Susan, and switchgrass.** All of them handle New Castle County's zone 7a–7b winters, humid summers, and clay soil without spraying, fussing, or constant watering once established. Here's the full shortlist we plant again and again — because it works.

## What "low-maintenance" actually requires in Delaware

Delaware punishes plants three ways: heavy clay soil that stays wet in winter, humid summers that breed fungus on the wrong species, and — in wooded areas like Hockessin, Greenville, and Pike Creek — relentless deer. A genuinely low-maintenance plant here has to tolerate all three or be placed where its weakness doesn't matter. That's the filter behind every pick below.

## Shrubs that carry the landscape

- **Inkberry holly (Ilex glabra).** Native, evergreen, deer mostly ignore it, tolerates wet clay. The modern replacement for boxwood in soggy spots. 'Shamrock' and 'Gem Box' stay tidy.
- **Oakleaf hydrangea (Hydrangea quercifolia).** Native, huge summer blooms, burgundy fall color, peeling winter bark — four seasons from one shrub, and it shrugs off shade.
- **Itea 'Little Henry' (Virginia sweetspire).** Native, fragrant June flowers, scarlet fall color, and it *likes* the wet clay that kills other shrubs. We use it constantly in drainage-adjacent beds.
- **Viburnum (arrowwood or doublefile).** Tough, birds love the berries, and deer browse it less than they browse hollies' tastier neighbors.
- **Boxwood** — still the king of formal structure and truly deer-proof, but give it drainage; it sulks in standing water.

## Perennials that come back stronger

- **Catmint (Nepeta 'Walker's Low').** Blooms May to frost if sheared once, laughs at drought, and deer and rabbits won't touch it.
- **Coneflower (Echinacea purpurea).** Native, pollinator magnet, blooms through July heat.
- **Black-eyed Susan (Rudbeckia 'Goldsturm').** The most reliable color in a Delaware August, period.
- **Hellebore.** Evergreen foliage, flowers in February when nothing else moves, completely deer-proof. The shade answer.
- **Amsonia (bluestar).** Spring flowers, golden fall foliage, zero pests. Criminally underused.

## Grasses instead of high-maintenance groundcover

- **Switchgrass (Panicum 'Northwind').** Native, vertical, stands through winter storms without flopping.
- **Little bluestem.** Native, copper fall color, thrives in the poor compacted soil of new construction lots in Middletown and Bear.
- **Fountain grass 'Hameln'.** The tidy front-of-bed workhorse.

Ornamental grasses need exactly one task per year: cut down in March. That's the whole maintenance calendar.

## The deer-pressure caveat

If you're in Hockessin, Greenville, or anywhere backing to woods, skip hostas, daylilies, azaleas, and arborvitae — they're deer candy. Stick to the deer-resistant picks above (catmint, hellebore, boxwood, inkberry, grasses) or plan on repellent spraying forever. ## Planting them so they're actually low-maintenance

Low-maintenance is mostly decided on planting day:

1. **Amend the clay** — compost worked into the whole bed, not just the hole.
2. **Plant high** — root flare at or slightly above grade; clay-buried crowns rot.
3. **Mulch 2–3 inches**, kept off the stems.
4. **Water deeply the first season** — after year one, the list above largely fends for itself.
